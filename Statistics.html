The statistics, explained

The map shows patterns: groups of divisions, gaps from the planned routes, shifts at the phase lines. This page asks of each pattern: could it be a coincidence? Each question starts with a plain answer, then the numbers, then a fold-out explaining how it was worked out. Every term is defined in the glossary.

AAre the groups closer to the planned routes than chance?

Yes. The groups of divisions found from their movements follow the planned AGS lines of advance much more closely than the same divisions put into random groups.

In 1,000 tries, none of the 1,000 random groupings came as close. How this was worked out

Each planned line of advance (digitized from a planning map, ±5–10 km) is paired with the group whose average path is nearest overall (least total Fréchet distance, one group per line). The gap between them is measured every 10 km along the planned line and averaged.

Null hypothesis: the groups tell us nothing about where divisions went relative to the plan. Test: permutation test — the divisions were shuffled between groups of the same sizes 1,000 times, and each time the average paths, the pairing and the gap were recomputed. The p-value is the share of shuffles at least as close as the real groups.

The 0–100 scores on the app's plan cards are reading aids tied to the ~20 km mapping error; they are not tests. This is the test.

How this was worked out

Each division's planned formation (6th Army, III Panzer Corps, Panzer Group 1 without III Corps, 17th Army) comes from the researched unit roster; 34 divisions could be identified (17 could not and are left out). A table counts how many divisions of each formation fell in each group.

Null hypothesis: group and formation are unrelated. Test: exact permutation test — the formation labels were shuffled 10,000 times; the p-value is the share of shuffles with as strong an association. This is valid with the small counts here, where the usual chi-square formula is not.

How this was worked out

The directional-vector groups come from k-means on each division's end point and the centre of its path. Are they real? The silhouette of the real groups was compared with 1,000 made-up data sets with no groups, spread evenly over the same area (null hypothesis: no grouping structure). How many? The gap statistic (Tibshirani et al. 2001) and the silhouette were computed for 2–6 groups. Stable? The divisions were resampled 300 times and regrouped; each group's overlap with its best match is its Jaccard stability (Hennig 2007).

The silhouette favours few groups and the gap statistic more, so the data do not settle the number: the published 5 and the one-per-line 4 (which sets aside divisions that moved under 200 km) are both defensible choices, to be reported as choices.

DDid the front shift at each phase line?

Yes at lines 1, 2, 4. After crossing each line, the weight of the advance sat further south than before. At phase 3 most divisions did move south (median 20 km), but a minority moved a long way north, so the test, which weighs how far each division moved, cannot rule out chance.

How this was worked out

For each division, its average north–south position in the stretch before a phase line and in the stretch after it. One pair per division, so the same division is compared with itself.

Null hypothesis: no consistent north–south shift. Test: Wilcoxon signed-rank test on the paired differences; the 95% range of the median shift is a bootstrap interval (300 resamples).

How this was worked out

On each phase line's planned date, every division is placed on one line: its distance along the general direction of the whole advance (the bearing from the divisions' average 22 June position to their average last position). Jenks natural breaks cut that line into the fewest bins that explain at least 90% of the spread; only then is each bin read against the phase line.

Null hypothesis: the divisions were scattered at random along that stretch. Test: the share of the stretch left empty at the breaks (from the last division before each break to the first after it), against 1,000 random scatters of as many positions over the same range, cut into the same number of bins. The goodness of variance fit is no test here: scattered positions reach about 0.95 with four bins too.

Dates for phases 3–5 read vague planning dates: 22 July for mid-July, 25 July for after mid-July, 30 July for late July.

Glossary

p-value
How often a pattern at least this strong would turn up by chance if the thing being tested were not there. Small = unlikely to be luck. It does not say how big or important the effect is.
Significant
A p-value below 0.05 after the Holm correction: by the usual research convention, the pattern is unlikely to be chance.
Holm correction
Running 20 tests makes a lucky result more likely somewhere. The Holm method raises each p-value to allow for this; the page reports the corrected values.
Permutation test
Shuffle the labels many times (for example, put divisions into random groups) and see how often the shuffled data match as well as the real data. The share of shuffles that do is the p-value. It makes no assumptions about bell curves.
Null hypothesis
The 'nothing is going on' explanation the test tries to rule out, such as 'the groups are no closer to the plan than random groups'.
Bootstrap / 95% interval
Re-draw the divisions at random, with replacement, hundreds of times and redo the calculation each time. The middle 95% of the results is the range the number would plausibly fall in with a different sample.
Fréchet distance
The distance between two routes, like the shortest leash that lets a person walk one route and a dog the other, both only moving forward. Used to pair each planned line with its nearest group.
Mean route gap
The average distance between a planned line and its paired group's average path, measured every 10 km along the planned line.
Cramér's V
How strongly two groupings go together, from 0 (unrelated) to 1 (identical). Here: the computer's groups and the planned formations. Above 0.5 is strong.
Adjusted Rand index
Another measure of agreement between two groupings: 0 = no better than chance, 1 = identical.
Silhouette
How well each division fits its own group compared with the next-nearest group, from −1 to 1. Above 0.5 = clearly separate groups; 0.25–0.5 = fairly separate.
Gap statistic
Compares how tight the groups are with groups found in made-up data that has no groups at all, for each possible number of groups; picks the smallest number after which adding groups stops helping.
Made-up data with no groups
Sets of random points spread evenly over the same area (1,000 for the tested number of groups, 100 for each other number), used as the 'no real groups' benchmark.
Jaccard stability
Resample the divisions, regroup, and measure how much each group overlaps its best match (0–1). 0.75 or more = stable; 0.6–0.75 = a real pattern but less sharp; below 0.5 = falls apart.
Wilcoxon signed-rank test
Compares each division's position before and after a line, one pair per division, and asks whether the moves lean one way more than chance would allow. Bigger moves count for more.
Median shift
The middle value of the divisions' north–south moves at a line: half moved more, half less.
Spearman's ρ (rho)
Whether divisions kept their order from north to south, from −1 (completely reversed) to 1 (same order). Above 0.8 = very strong.
Jenks natural breaks
Splits values into groups so each group is as tight as possible. Here the breaks fall where few division-days were spent, i.e. ground the divisions crossed quickly.
Goodness of variance fit
How much of the spread the natural-break bands explain (0–1). The stretch bands use the fewest bands reaching 0.8, the planned-date bins the fewest reaching 0.9. A high fit alone does not show clumps: randomly scattered points reach about 0.95 with four bins.
Past / on the line / short
A natural-break bin read against the phase line: every division in it past the line, some past and some short of it, or none past it.
10 km rule
A division counts as having reached a phase line if it is past it or within 10 km of it on the planned date.
Mix (normalized entropy)
How mixed a group is by planned formation, from 0 (one formation only) to 1 (all four in equal numbers). Divisions of unknown formation are left out.
Division-days
One division at one place for one day, weighted by strength (a one-tenth detachment counts a tenth).

Limits — please keep with the results

  • Small numbers. 44–51 divisions. With so few, "not significant" is weak evidence that there is no effect.
  • Divisions did not move independently. Divisions of one corps moved together under one order, so there are effectively fewer independent cases than divisions. Shuffling single divisions can make results look stronger than they are; treat p-values near 0.05 with care.
  • The inputs carry error. Hand-drawn planned lines (±5–10 km), georeferenced maps (3–40 km) and merged positions (~17 km). Gaps under about 20 km cannot be told apart from zero.
  • Unknown formation for 17 of 51 divisions, left out of question B.
  • Choices that are not tests. The number of groups, the 200 km "stayed behind" cut, the 50 km corridor and the app's 0–100 scores are modelling or presentation choices. Question C reports how well the data support the number of groups.
  • Several tests at once. The 20 p-values in the tables are corrected (Holm); the supporting numbers (bearing differences, adjusted Rand) are not.
